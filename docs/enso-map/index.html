What the map shows

For each land cell (0.25°, about 28 km) and each three-month season, the correlation r between that season’s rainfall and the Niño3.4 index, 1981–2025 (44 or 45 years, depending on the season and the lead time). Positive r (blue) means the season has tended to be wetter in El Niño years and drier in La Niña years; negative r (brown) the reverse.

The darker shades (|r| ≥ 0.5) mark places where ENSO accounts for at least a quarter of the year-to-year variation in that season’s rain; the lighter ones (0.3 to 0.5) for roughly a tenth to a quarter. Grey: no clear relationship (|r| below 0.3, or not significant at p < 0.05). Hatched: these three months bring less than a quarter of the year’s rain in that cell (or under 0.25 mm a day), so the season is not assessed.

The box for a cell also shows its 12 seasons side by side, so the seasons when ENSO matters there are visible at a glance.

How it is computed

  • Rain: ERA5 monthly precipitation, averaged over the three months of the season, one value per year and cell.
  • ENSO: NOAA’s monthly Niño3.4 anomaly (ERSST v5, 1991–2020 base), averaged over three months, either the same three months or a window ending one, two or three months earlier. The map uses whichever of these four gives the largest |r|; the box says which.
  • Which seasons count: a season is assessed in a cell only if it brings at least 25% of the year’s rain there (at least its share if rain were spread evenly over the year) and averages at least 0.25 mm a day. Where rain falls all year, as in much of Indonesia, this skips some seasons that are still wet. Drier months: Show colours those seasons too, hatched, wherever they average at least 0.25 mm a day; the box always gives their r.
  • Colour: Pearson r, two-tailed p < 0.05 and |r| ≥ 0.3. “All values” shows every r in assessed seasons, significant or not.

This is the method of the global teleconnection survey. The survey’s pixel ENSO map shows each cell’s strongest season; this map shows every season. On the cells both maps cover, taking the strongest season here reproduces the survey’s map exactly.

Reading it with care

  • Each cell is computed on its own, without smoothing. Even with no ENSO effect, about one cell in twenty would pass p < 0.05 by chance (somewhat more, because the best of four lead times is kept), so trust patches of the same colour, not isolated cells.
  • Choosing the best of four lead times makes r slightly larger than a single fixed lead would.
  • The correlation describes 1981–2025. It is not a forecast, and it says nothing about how large a particular El Niño’s effect will be.
  • ERA5 rainfall comes from a weather model constrained by observations. Where gauges are few it can differ from satellite and station records; check CHIRPS or station data before relying on one area.
  • Coverage: all land between 60°S and 84°N. The survey’s maps stop at 100°W, 36°S and 56°N. Coastlines are Natural Earth 1:10m with minor islands, so small islands have cells; coastal cells mix land and sea.
  • The map uses the Equal Earth projection, which shows every region at its true relative size (the projection the UN General Assembly encouraged in September 2026). Countries that cross 180°, such as Fiji and Kiribati, appear at both edges.

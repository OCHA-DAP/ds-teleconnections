El Niño 2026/27

Haiti spring 2027 rainfall outlook

An early indication of the March–June 2027 rains (the printemps season) for drought anticipatory action readiness, nationally and by department. It compares what a typical spring brings with the range we expect for 2027, drawing on the El Niño record, our own statistical models and other providers' seasonal forecasts.

OCHA Centre for Humanitarian Data · 6 November 2026 · built on the seasonal forecasts started in November 2026. The forecasts listed under what to watch will update every figure here.

Low–moderateconfidence in this outlook. The El Niño link is moderately well supported, but the forecasts have weak skill and this event is beyond the record.
  • The seasonal total leans normal to wet. Every line of evidence centres between 105% and 125% of a typical spring. The likely range for 2027 sits about a tenth higher than in a typical year and is just as wide, so a dry season is not ruled out.
  • The evidence is consistent but not independent. All of it depends on how Haiti's spring responds after a strong El Niño. Forecasts made in November have weak skill for Haiti: correlations of 0.15–0.3 with observed rainfall. This El Niño is already stronger than any in our record at the same time of year and is forecast to grow, so every method is extrapolating.
  • There is no reliable early signal on the timing of the rains. After strong El Niño winters, onset dates were close to normal on average, with large scatter. Long dry spells were much less common in the three most recent cases (2010, 2016, 2024) and close to average in 1983, 1992 and 1998. False starts (rains that begin and then break for ten days or more) were more common, but this result is tentative.
  • The southern peninsula is the least certain. In Sud, Grande'Anse and Nippes, spring rain tracks winter Niño3.4 only weakly in CHIRPS and hardly at all in ERA5, and springs 1983 and 1998 were below typical there in CHIRPS. SEAS5 meanwhile puts the south at its wettest.
  • For readiness: nothing available today points to a drier-than-typical spring, so a forecast-based readiness activation for drought is not supported. The documented risk is badly distributed rain on top of the 2026 drought. The 1992 and 2016 droughts both came in years whose spring totals were above average. Plan around observed onset and dry spells from April to June, and recheck this outlook at each date listed below.

How the combined outlook is built: the central value is the average of the central estimates from the two sets of past springs, the two regressions and SEAS5. The likely range averages the 10th and 90th percentiles of the two regressions and SEAS5. Departments use the same recipe. Adding NMME would move the national figure to 113% (83–152%). The regressions take the 2026/27 winter Niño3.4 as 2.7 ± 0.45 on our index (see the next section); a winter of 2.3 or 3.0 moves their centre by about 2 points. In cross-validation, the regressions and past-spring comparisons barely beat simply assuming a typical year, which is why the 2027 ranges are as wide as the typical range.

Other providers point the same way. CPC's NMME maps show a weak wet anomaly over Hispaniola for March–May and April–June. FEWS NET's October forecast review says models suggest "a shift to above average rainfall in early 2027", while noting that in past El Niño years the most frequent outcome was a dry start in April–June. The October CariCOF newsletter expects a "potentially earlier (but intense)" onset in April and leaves Haiti blank on its maps. The CFSv2 run from October tilts above normal for February–April. In our records the dry starts came when El Niño was developing or weak (2015, 2019, 2020). Springs after strong winters were not dry; that is our reading of the FEWS NET statement.

Why El Niño matters, and why this year is at the edge of the record

An El Niño tends to dry Haiti while it develops and to wet the early rainy season in the spring after it peaks. The second effect works through the tropical North Atlantic, which warms 2–4 months after the El Niño peak and adds moisture over the Greater Antilles. The effect is strongest in May–June (Chen and Taylor 2002; Giannini, Kushnir and Cane 2000). The tropical North Atlantic is already warm: +0.64 °C in September (NOAA PSL index).

The teleconnections survey grades Haiti's El Niño signal as robust: drier in May–July while El Niño develops (Jury, Malmgren and Winter 2007; FEWS NET). 2015 and 2026 fit that pattern. This page looks at the spring after the peak, where the evidence is more limited.

National March–June rainfall in CHIRPS (% of the 1991–2020 average) against the December–February Niño3.4 anomaly (ERSSTv6, 1981–2010 base). The dashed line marks the driest third of springs. Hover over a point for ERA5's value.

The relationship is not a straight line. Springs with weak warm winters (Niño3.4 +0.5 to +1.0) were among the driest: 2015 (51% of typical), 2020 (61%) and 2019 (67%). All six springs after strong winters stayed out of the driest third in CHIRPS, and five of six did in ERA5. That is why a straight-line regression gains little, and why the past-spring comparison is the more direct evidence.

This winter will be stronger than any of the six. Niño3.4 was +2.09 in September on our index, above September 1997 (+1.63) and September 2015 (+1.67), the two strongest events in the record. CPC (8 October) expects a strong to very strong event through January–March. The NMME runs from November average +3.3 for December–February (80% range 2.7–3.9 on a 1991–2020 base) and still +1.9 in March–May, a slower decay than any past case (+0.5 to +1.1). In the past cases, the speed of decay did not line up with spring rain: 1992 and 2016 decayed slowest and were wet. We therefore make no adjustment for it, but a lingering event is untested ground.

For the regressions and the shaded band in the chart, the 2026/27 winter is taken as 2.7 ± 0.45 on our index. That sits between two references: September's +2.09 plus the 0.1–0.4 °C that 1997 and 2015 added by the winter (about +2.2 to +2.5), and the NMME average (+3.3 on its own base, somewhat higher on ours).

How far the evidence agrees

Both records: winter Niño3.4 and spring rain correlate at 0.15 or more in CHIRPS and in ERA5. CHIRPS only: 0.20 or more in CHIRPS, under 0.10 in ERA5. Least certain: under 0.10 in ERA5 and under 0.20 in CHIRPS, with 1983 and 1998 below typical in CHIRPS.

Reading these maps

The departments differ far more in how much rain they normally get (about 230 mm in Nord-Ouest, 630 mm in Grande'Anse) than in the expected change for 2027 (+5% to +14% everywhere).

In the north and centre, spring rain tracks winter Niño3.4 in both rainfall records (r 0.15–0.22). In Ouest and Sud-Est it does in CHIRPS (r 0.21–0.24) but hardly in ERA5 (under 0.10), although most past strong El Niño springs were above typical in both.

In the southern peninsula the records and the models disagree. In ERA5, spring rain hardly tracks winter Niño3.4 there (r 0.02–0.07). In CHIRPS, springs 1983 and 1998 were below typical (Sud 95% and 82%, Grande'Anse 84% and 78%, Nippes 98% and 89%). Yet SEAS5 places Sud and Nippes at the 98th percentile of its history.

By department: onset and dry spells

Onset is the first day from 1 March with at least 1 mm that starts three days totalling 20 mm or more, as long as no dry spell of ten days or more (days under 1 mm) follows within 30 days. If one does, the start counts as a false start. Dry spells are counted from onset to 30 June. Metrics are computed on each 5 km CHIRPS cell for 1981–2026 and then averaged by area; IMERG (1998–2025) is used as a check. Reading these maps

None of the onset shifts is outside what chance alone would give with six cases.

The drop in long dry spells mostly reflects the wetter totals, and it comes from 2010, 2016 and 2024. In 1983, 1992 and 1998 dry spells were close to average.

False starts rose in five of the six past cases even after allowing for the wetter totals. This is the one timing signal, and it is tentative.

  • Onset timing: no robust signal either way. Every shift is under two standard errors from zero; Grande'Anse's +13 days comes mostly from 1983 (+50 days). In Nord and Centre, most of the area started late in 3 of the 6 past cases, against about 1 spring in 5 normally, which is within chance for six cases. The two datasets also disagree on individual years. CHIRPS has Nord and Artibonite starting late in 1992 (4 and 3 June), before the IMERG record begins. IMERG has Artibonite late in 1998, and Nord-Est, Artibonite and Nord-Ouest late in 2024; CHIRPS shows neither. The same onset rule gives a national date of 9 May in CHIRPS and about 20 April in IMERG, so the maps show shifts only.
  • Long dry spells: less common after strong winters on average. The drop comes from 2010, 2016 and 2024 (national shares of 5–15% against 32% normally). 1983, 1992 and 1998 were close to average (26–30%). Most of the difference follows from the wetter totals, so it offers no reassurance for a year like 1998.
  • False starts: more common after strong winters (3.4 against 2.3 per season nationally). After allowing for the wetter totals, the increase holds in five of the six years (2016 was flat), which would fit early rains that start and stop. Treat this as tentative: day-to-day timing is the weakest part of CHIRPS, and we have not yet checked it in IMERG. For farmers who plant on the first rains, it is the risk most worth watching.
  • Nord-Ouest often has no onset by 30 June under this rule, so its onset figures are unreliable.

What the impact record adds

EM-DAT lists six Haitian droughts since 1980. The three with a million or more people affected are April 1992 (1.0 million), January 2014 to July 2015 (1.0 million), and January 2016 to February 2017 (3.6 million, spread across all ten departments). Two of these three came with strong El Niño winters (1991/92 and 2015/16), and in both years spring rainfall totals were above average (CHIRPS 111% in 1992 and 113% in 2016). Our monthly checks point to distribution and carry-over instead: a dry March 2016 in the interior, dry Junes in both years, and in 2016 the aftermath of the failed 2015 spring (51% of typical). A trigger on the seasonal total alone would have missed both. A smaller record, a 1980–83 drought in the southwest (about 103,000 affected), runs into 1983, a post-El Niño spring in which CHIRPS has Grande'Anse at 84% and Sud at 95% of typical. It touches the same departments we rate least certain.

2026 gives the same warning. The spring total was near typical (CHIRPS 105%, ERA5 95%), but late onsets and long dry spells covered more of the country than usual (39% and 40% of the area, against 28% and 32%), and May–August then ran at about 30–70% of typical. The 2027 spring starts on that stress. CariCOF also flags record heat, which raises water demand even in a near-typical rainy season.

  2. About 13–19 NovIRI seasonal forecast with its first March–May map, and the IRI ENSO plume.

  4. Early DecSEAS5 December run: our first SEAS5 forecast that covers all of March–June.
  5. 10 DecC3S multi-model forecast: first March–May.
  6. 10 Jan 2027C3S April–June and June.
  7. Jan – early AprWinter North Atlantic Oscillation (January–March). A positive NAO is the main documented route to a dry early season (Martinez et al. 2020). Here it correlates at r = −0.37 with May–June rain, the strongest single predictor we found.
  8. Early MarSEAS5 March run: the first with useful skill for March–May (r ≈ 0.48 nationally, 0.28–0.52 by department).
  9. Apr – JunObserved onset, false starts and dry spells by department (CHIRPS and IMERG). This is where the risk sits.

What would change this outlook: a forecast that moves April–June toward the dry end of the typical range in C3S or SEAS5, a strongly positive winter NAO, or an El Niño that lingers well beyond spring.

Implications for a readiness trigger

  • A forecast-based readiness activation for a dry spring is not supported by any current line of evidence. None points to a drier-than-typical season.
  • The historical case for action rests on distribution. An observational trigger on late onset, false starts or dry spells in April–May, by department, fits the impact record (1992, 2016) better than one on seasonal totals. Its return period would need to be computed and validated against two records: the rainfall metrics built for this note and the impact record (EM-DAT, FEWS NET and IPC).
  • A readiness step tied to the March forecasts and to early onset monitoring would cost little if the season turns out wet.

Data, methods and caveats

Rainfall: CHIRPS v2.0, monthly and daily for February–July, 1981–2026 (IRI Data Library). ERA5 and SEAS5 by department from the team's admin-1 statistics; IMERG from the team's daily statistics, 1998–2025. NMME from the IRI Data Library: CanESM5, GEM5.2-NEMO, CESM1, CCSM4 and CFSv2, all started in November 2026 and all reaching June 2027. GEOS-S2S and SPEAR had no November 2026 run yet. Indices: Niño3.4 (ERSSTv6, 1981–2010 base, NOAA PSL), NAO (CPC), tropical North Atlantic (PSL). Department averages are weighted by area with fractional cell coverage.

Definitions: typical means the 1991–2020 average; each dataset is compared with its own average. Likely ranges run from the 10th to the 90th percentile. Strong El Niño winters have a December–February Niño3.4 of at least +1.0: 1983, 1992, 1998, 2010, 2016 and 2024. The regressions relate log rainfall to winter Niño3.4. NMME and SEAS5 ranges come from a model of observed rainfall given the forecast, scaled by each system's correlation with observations.

Datasets disagree: CHIRPS and ERA5 correlate at only 0.65 for the national spring total and disagree on key years: 1998 (105% vs 91%) and 2019 (67% vs 101%). ERA5 is about 25% drier than CHIRPS overall. The interior and the southern peninsula are where they differ most. Millimetre figures on this page are on the CHIRPS scale.

IMERG 2026 is left out: its values run at about 2.4 times CHIRPS, against 1.2–1.6 in earlier years, which looks like a break in the record rather than real rain.

EM-DAT is used under its non-commercial terms. Only dates and totals are summarised here.
